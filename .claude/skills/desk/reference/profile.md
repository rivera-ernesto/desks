# The user's profile

A Desk works better when it knows who it works for: what they are trying to do, how they write,
who the people around them are, and what they never want done. Without that, every session asks
again or guesses.

**Do not reinvent what Claude already has.** Preferences about *how to work with them* ("short
answers", "ask before installing") belong in Claude's own memory or their `CLAUDE.md`. The profile
is for what is about *them*, and it is theirs: a plain file they can read and edit.

## Where

One file, `profile.md`, in the Desk root by default (path recorded as `- Profile:` in the Desk
block). If the folder is shared or public, keep it outside the folder instead
(`~/.config/desk/profile.md`) and say why. **Never** put it in a review packet, and add the names
in it to the private terms.

## Building it (`desk profile`, about five minutes)

Ask, one at a time, and accept "skip":

1. What are you working toward this year, in a sentence or two?
2. What do you want this to take off your hands, and what do you want to keep doing yourself?
3. Who will come up often? First name and who they are to you. (Relationships are easy to get
   wrong and costly when wrong: always check here before writing "your partner" or "your client".)
4. Paste something you wrote that sounds like you. (For drafts in their voice.)
5. What should I never do without asking, beyond the usual five?
6. Is there a question you ask yourself before deciding things? (Their own test, in their words.
   Apply it for them when a decision comes up. Do not invent one.)

Write short sections: *Aims · Mine to keep · People · Voice · Never without asking · My tests*.

## Starting from something that exists

Most people have already written this somewhere: an about page, a values list, a manifesto for a
project, instructions they gave another assistant. If they point at one (a file or a link), read
it, fill the sections it covers, show them the result, and ask only for what is missing. Keep
their words; do not improve them.

A project or a community can have such a statement too (what it is for, what it will not do, the
questions it asks before acting). That belongs to the project, not to the person: put it at the
top of that topic's README, and leave the personal profile about the person.

## Using it

Read it when the topic touches them, their people or their voice. Not at every opening.

## Learning as you go

At a close, look back at how the session went: what they corrected, what they always asked for,
what they ignored. If something would hold next time, **offer it as a question, not a verdict**:

> "You moved the price down in all three drafts before I suggested it. Want me to start lower by
> default?"

Not: "You undervalue your work." On a yes, add one dated line to the profile or to the topic's
"What we learned". On a no or silence, drop it. Never write an interpretation of the person into
the file; write what they asked for.
